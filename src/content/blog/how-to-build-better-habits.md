Habit advice online is often confident and short on evidence. This guide sticks to ideas with reasonable support and says where the evidence is thin.

## What a habit is

A habit is a behavior that has become fairly automatic in a given context. It is triggered by a cue (a time, a place, a previous action) more than by deciding in the moment. Building a habit means repeating a behavior in a consistent context until it takes less effort.

## 1. Start tiny

Small actions are easier to repeat, and repetition is what builds automaticity. Behavior-design researchers such as BJ Fogg and writers such as James Clear popularized starting with very small versions: one push-up, two minutes, one page. You can scale up later. The point is to make starting easy.

## 2. Use an if-then plan

*"When [cue], I will [action]."* A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 studies found that these specific "implementation intentions" made people more likely to follow through than goals alone. Pick a cue that already happens: finishing breakfast, sitting at your desk, getting into bed.

## 3. Keep the context consistent

The more steady the cue and place, the faster the link forms. Doing a habit at a random time in random places is possible but slower.

## 4. Track honestly

Tracking makes the behavior visible. It works best when the definition of done is clear and logging takes seconds. See [how to track habits effectively](/blog/how-to-track-habits-effectively/) and try the [online habit tracker](/online-habit-tracker/).

## 5. Expect it to take longer than 21 days

A University College London study of 96 people found it took between 18 and 254 days for a new behavior to feel automatic, with a median of 66 days. It varied widely between people and between habits. Missing a single day did not appear to derail the process. The study was small, so treat the numbers as a rough guide.

## 6. Build one habit at a time

Each new habit competes for attention. Start with one, or two at most. Add another after the first feels routine. There is no research-backed magic number, but fewer is easier.

## 7. Make good habits easy and bad habits hard

Change the environment: put the book on the pillow, the gym bag by the door. For habits you want to cut back, add friction: delete the app, keep sweets out of sight, or set a limit goal and track it.

## 8. Plan for failure

Decide in advance what you will do on a bad day. Use a smaller version, and avoid missing twice. See [how to stay consistent with habits](/blog/how-to-stay-consistent-with-habits/).

## 9. Reward the behavior

Enjoyment helps repetition. Pair the habit with something you like, such as a favorite podcast on a walk, or take a moment to notice that you did it.
